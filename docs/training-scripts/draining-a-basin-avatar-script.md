## SPOKEN SCRIPT

Draining a fountain basin is routine work. It is also one of the easiest jobs on our
list to turn into a return visit, and almost always for the same two reasons. This will
take about four minutes, and by the end you will know the order the job runs in and the
two checks that stop a callback.

Start with the pump. Before you open a valve, before you move any water at all, isolate
the pump at the panel and confirm it is off.

Here is why that comes first. As the basin drains, the water level drops below the
suction. If the pump is still live, it keeps running and it starts pulling air. A pump
running dry destroys its own seal in minutes. Not hours. Minutes. And once that seal is
gone, a routine drain has become a second visit with a part on order, and a customer
waiting.

Now, the part that catches people out. Switching the timer off is not isolation.

A timer that happens to be off right now is still a timer. It can come on while you are
standing in the basin with your hands in the strainer housing. It does not know you are
there. So go to the panel, isolate it properly, and then confirm the pump does not
respond before you open anything at all. Confirming is part of the step. Isolating and
assuming is not the same as isolating and checking.

With the pump isolated, there are two more things to settle before a drop of water
moves.

First, know where the drain actually runs to, and know it can take the volume you are
about to send it. Second, look at what is downstream. Planters. Walkways. A neighbouring
basement. Neither of those takes a minute to check, and both have caused real problems
on real sites.

Now you can drain.

Open the drain valve slowly. Fast is not faster. Opening it wide stirs up every bit of
sediment sitting on the bottom, and it can surge whatever the drain runs into. Slow and
steady gets you to the same place with less mess and no surprises.

Let the basin fall to empty on its own. You do not need to chase the last of it with a
bucket unless you have a specific reason to.

While it is empty, take the opportunity. This is the only time you can see any of it
properly. Look at the suction strainer. Look at the seals. Look at the state of the
surfaces. Five minutes here is worth more than any inspection you will do with the basin
full.

Now the important part. Before you refill, there are two things you must check, and it
is both of them, not one or the other.

The first is the suction strainer. It has to be clear of debris. Anything sitting in
there gets pulled straight into the pump the moment you restart it, and that is what
takes out a seal.

The second is the drain valve. It has to be fully closed. Not nearly closed. Fully. A
half closed valve looks closed from where you are standing, and it will quietly refill
straight to waste. You will spend twenty minutes wondering why the level will not hold
before you work out what is happening.

Both of those. Every time. That is the difference between finishing the job and coming
back to it.

Then refill to the normal operating level. Not partway, and not up to the overflow. The
normal running level.

Once you are there, bring the pump back on, and stay with it. Listen to it long enough
to hear it prime properly. You want a steady sound, not a gulping one. If it sounds like
it is drawing air, stop, and go back and check the level and the strainer again. It will
not settle on its own, and leaving it running while you hope is how you lose the seal
you just protected.

So, the whole job in order. Isolate at the panel and confirm. Check where the water is
going. Open the valve slowly and let it empty. Inspect while you can see. Check the
strainer and check the valve, both of them. Refill to operating level. Restart, and
listen.

Get that order right and this is a thirty minute job that stays done. Thank you.
